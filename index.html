Module 1: Radiation Physics Foundations

Foundations

1. Nature of Matter

Before we can understand X-rays, we first need to understand what they pass through. That brings us to matter.

Definition: Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. Everything around us, including the human body, air, water, and X-ray equipment, is made of matter. Matter exists in three main states: solid, liquid, and gas.

Example: Bones are solids, blood is a liquid, and air in the lungs is a gas.
Clinical Relevance: Radiographers need to understand matter because X-rays interact differently with different types of matter. Bones absorb more X-rays than soft tissues, while air absorbs the least. This difference produces the contrast seen on radiographs.
Summary: Matter is anything with mass and volume, and all body tissues are forms of matter that interact differently with X-rays.
Foundations

2. Atomic Structure

Definition: An atom is the smallest unit of an element that retains its chemical properties. It consists of a nucleus containing protons (positive charge) and neutrons (no charge), while electrons (negative charge) move around the nucleus in energy shells. Most interactions between X-rays and matter involve the electrons.

Example: The tungsten target inside the X-ray tube is made of tungsten atoms. When fast-moving electrons strike these atoms, X-rays are produced.
Clinical Relevance: Understanding atomic structure helps explain how X-rays are produced, how ionization occurs, and why different tissues absorb radiation differently.
Summary: Atoms are the basic building blocks of matter, and their electrons play a major role in X-ray production and radiation interactions.
Foundations

3. Electromagnetic Spectrum

Definition: The electromagnetic spectrum is the complete range of electromagnetic waves arranged according to their wavelength and frequency. It includes radio waves, microwaves, infrared rays, visible light, ultraviolet rays, X-rays, and gamma rays. X-rays have short wavelengths and high frequencies, giving them enough energy to penetrate the body.

  • Long wavelength = Low frequency = Low energy
  • Short wavelength = High frequency = High energy
Example: Visible light allows us to see objects, while X-rays allow radiographers to see structures inside the body.
Clinical Relevance: Radiographers use the X-ray portion of the electromagnetic spectrum because it provides enough energy to produce diagnostic images while allowing exposure to be carefully controlled.
Summary: The electromagnetic spectrum contains all electromagnetic waves, with high-energy X-rays used for medical imaging.
Foundations

4. Ionization

Definition: Ionization is the process by which an atom gains or loses electrons, becoming electrically charged. In diagnostic radiography, X-rays have enough energy to remove electrons from atoms, creating positively charged ions. This process can lead to biological effects in living tissues.

Example: When an X-ray photon strikes a body cell and removes an electron, ionization has occurred.
Clinical Relevance: Ionization is responsible for both the formation of X-ray images and potential tissue damage, making radiation protection essential.
Summary: Ionization occurs when radiation removes an electron from an atom, creating an ion.
Foundations

5. Types of Radiation

Definition: Radiation is energy travelling from one place to another. It is divided into Electromagnetic Radiation (waves without mass, like X-rays) and Particulate Radiation (particles with mass, like alpha and beta particles). Radiation is also classified as Ionizing or Non-Ionizing.

Example: X-rays are ionizing electromagnetic radiation, while visible light is non-ionizing radiation.
Clinical Relevance: Radiographers work mainly with ionizing radiation because it can produce images but also has the potential to damage tissues if not properly controlled.
Summary: Radiation may travel as waves or particles and can be ionizing or non-ionizing.
Foundations

6. Units of Radiation

Definition: Radiation is measured using different units depending on what is being assessed:

  • Becquerel (Bq): Measures the activity of a radioactive source.
  • Gray (Gy): Measures the absorbed dose of radiation in tissue.
  • Sievert (Sv): Measures the overall biological effect of radiation on living tissue.
Example: A patient's radiation dose during a procedure is commonly expressed in milligray (mGy) or millisievert (mSv).
Clinical Relevance: Knowledge of radiation units helps radiographers monitor patient doses, compare procedures, and ensure safe radiation practice.
Summary: Becquerel measures activity, Gray measures absorbed dose, and Sievert measures biological effect.

Module 2: X-ray Production & Equipment

X-ray Production

1. Cathode & Thermionic Emission

Heating the tungsten filament in the cathode releases free electrons into space via thermionic emission.

Interactive Simulation: Filament Heat Control
Electron Cloud Density: Low
Example: The filament in the cathode is heated before every exposure to release electrons.
Clinical Relevance: Increasing mAs increases filament heat, producing more electrons and a higher quantity of X-rays.
Summary: Cathode releases electrons; Anode converts their energy into X-rays and heat.
X-ray Production

2. Bremsstrahlung Radiation

Definition: "Bremsstrahlung" means braking radiation. Occurs when a high-speed electron passes near the nucleus of a tungsten atom, slows down, changes direction, and releases its lost energy as an X-ray photon.

Example: About 85% to 90% of diagnostic X-rays produced in the beam are Bremsstrahlung radiation.
Clinical Relevance: Bremsstrahlung radiation provides the majority of useful diagnostic photons in the primary beam.
Summary: Produced when fast electrons slow down and deflect near the tungsten nucleus.
X-ray Production

3. Characteristic Radiation

Definition: Occurs when a high-speed electron knocks out an inner-shell electron of a tungsten atom. An outer-shell electron drops down to fill the empty space, releasing the energy difference as an X-ray photon.

Example: Characteristic X-rays are produced when K-shell vacancies are filled in tungsten target atoms.

Basic Physics

7. Basic Electricity

Definition: Electricity is the movement of electrons through a conductor. An electric current flows when a voltage pushes electrons through a circuit. In an X-ray machine, electricity supplies energy to heat the filament and accelerate electrons toward the anode.

Example: Switching on an X-ray machine allows electric current to heat the cathode filament.
Clinical Relevance: Electricity is essential for producing X-rays and operating every component of the X-ray machine.
Summary: Electricity provides the pushing force (voltage) and electron movement needed for X-ray production.
Basic Physics

8. Magnetism

Definition: Magnetism is the force produced by moving electric charges. Electricity and magnetism are closely related because an electric current creates a magnetic field around its conductor.

Example: Electric motors inside rotating anode X-ray tubes spin using magnetic induction principles.
Clinical Relevance: Understanding magnetism explains how motors inside X-ray equipment operate and lays the foundation for MRI physics.
Summary: Moving electrons create magnetic fields, making electricity and magnetism closely related.
Basic Physics

9. Wave Properties

Definition: A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy. The main wave properties are Wavelength (distance between waves), Frequency (waves per second in Hz), Amplitude (wave height), and Velocity (speed of wave travel).

Example: X-rays have much shorter wavelengths and higher frequencies than visible light.
Clinical Relevance: Wave properties explain why X-rays can penetrate human body tissue while visible light cannot.
Summary: Wave properties determine how electromagnetic waves travel and interact with matter.
Basic Physics

10. Energy and Frequency

Definition: Frequency and energy are directly proportional. Higher-frequency waves carry higher energy. Because X-rays have very high frequencies, they carry enough energy to pass through soft tissues while being absorbed by dense bones.

Example: Gamma rays and X-rays have much higher frequencies and energies than radio waves or visible light.
Clinical Relevance: Higher-frequency beams provide greater penetration, directly relating to exposure factors like kVp.
Summary: Higher frequency means higher energy, giving X-rays their penetrating power.

Module 2: X-ray Production & Equipment

Production & Equipment

1. X-ray Tube

Definition: The X-ray tube is the sealed vacuum glass/metal tube where X-rays are generated. The vacuum prevents fast-moving electrons from colliding with air molecules before hitting the target.

Example: Every chest or extremity X-ray requires the X-ray tube to generate the primary beam.
Clinical Relevance: The X-ray tube is the heart of the system. If it fails or overheats, no diagnostic image can be produced.
Summary: The X-ray tube provides a vacuum environment where electrons are accelerated to create X-rays.
Production & Equipment

2. Cathode and Anode

Definition: The **Cathode** is the negative electrode containing a filament that releases electrons via *thermionic emission* when heated. The **Anode** is the positive electrode containing a tungsten target that attracts electrons and converts their kinetic energy into heat and X-rays.

Example: Millions of electrons travel from the heated cathode filament to strike the tungsten anode target during an exposure.
Clinical Relevance: Tungsten is used due to its high melting point and high atomic number, helping withstand intense heat.
Summary: Cathode releases electrons; Anode converts their energy into X-rays and heat.
Production & Equipment

3. Tube Housing

Definition: The protective lead-lined outer casing surrounding the X-ray tube. It contains insulating oil for cooling and electrical protection, absorbing leakage radiation so only the primary beam leaves through the tube window.

Example: About 99% of kinetic energy becomes heat; insulating oil absorbs this heat during routine procedures.
Clinical Relevance: Tube housing protects patients and radiographers from excessive heat buildup and off-focus leakage radiation.
Summary: Tube housing provides radiation shielding, oil cooling, and electrical protection.
Production & Equipment

4. Bremsstrahlung Radiation

Definition: "Bremsstrahlung" means braking radiation. Occurs when a high-speed electron passes near the nucleus of a tungsten atom, slows down, changes direction, and releases its lost energy as an X-ray photon.

Example: About 85% to 90% of diagnostic X-rays produced in the beam are Bremsstrahlung radiation.
Clinical Relevance: Bremsstrahlung radiation provides the majority of useful diagnostic photons in the primary beam.
Summary: Produced when fast electrons slow down and deflect near the tungsten nucleus.
Production & Equipment

5. Characteristic Radiation

Definition: Occurs when a high-speed electron knocks out an inner-shell electron of a tungsten atom. An outer-shell electron drops down to fill the empty space, releasing the energy difference as an X-ray photon.

Example: Characteristic X-rays are produced when K-shell vacancies are filled in tungsten target atoms.
Clinical Relevance: Contributes useful discrete energy photons to the diagnostic beam at higher kVp levels.
Summary: Produced when outer-shell electrons fill inner-shell vacancies in target atoms.
